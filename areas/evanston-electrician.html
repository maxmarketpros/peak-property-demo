Electrical Work in Evanston Homes

Evanston has the oldest housing stock anywhere we work. Victorians near the lake, foursquares and bungalows from the 1910s and 20s, brick two-flats along the main corridors — a big share of the city went up before 1930, which means it was wired before anyone imagined a house needing more than a few lights and a radio. We still open up Evanston walls and find original knob-and-tube and cloth-insulated wiring doing daily duty a hundred years on.

That old wiring has become more than a safety question. Insurance carriers have gotten strict about knob-and-tube, and plenty of Evanston homeowners first call us because their insurer is refusing to renew — or raising the premium — until it's replaced. The good news: rewiring an old house does not have to mean gutting it. We fish new wire through finished walls and ceilings, working around original plaster and trim instead of tearing through it.

Two-flats and condo conversions are their own story. Buildings split into units decades ago often still share undersized services and fuse boxes that were never meant for today's loads. We handle the whole fix — new panels, service upgrades up to 400A, and the City of Evanston permit paperwork that goes with it. And every service call includes a free once-over of your panel and wiring, so you know exactly what you're living with.

Common Electrical Problems We See in Evanston

  • Knob-and-tube wiring your insurer won't cover. So much of Evanston predates 1930 that carriers now routinely refuse or surcharge policies until the original wiring is replaced.
  • Cloth-insulated wiring that crumbles at the connections. The insulation dries out over decades and tends to fail first inside junction boxes and light fixtures, where you can't see it happening.
  • Fuse boxes and Federal Pacific panels still in service. We replace a lot of both in Evanston two-flats and older single-family homes — neither belongs in a house running modern appliances.
  • Undersized service in condo conversions. When an old building gets split into units, the original 60- or 100-amp service often gets divided up too, leaving each unit short on capacity.
  • Too few outlets, too many extension cords. Homes wired for one lamp per room end up with power strips daisy-chained behind furniture, overloading circuits that were marginal to begin with.
